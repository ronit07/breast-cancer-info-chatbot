## What BRCA1 and BRCA2 are
BRCA1 and BRCA2 make proteins that help repair damaged DNA. An inherited harmful change in one of these genes raises the risk of several cancers, most notably breast and ovarian cancer, and people with these changes often develop cancer at younger ages. Most carriers still have one working copy, which usually prevents cancer unless it is damaged or lost later.

## Breast cancer risk with a harmful BRCA change
More than 60% of women with an inherited harmful BRCA1 or BRCA2 change develop breast cancer in their lifetime, compared with about 13% of women in the general population. Breast cancer survivors with a BRCA1 change: about 30% to 40% develop cancer in the opposite breast within 20 years; for BRCA2 about 25%; general population about 8%. Men with a BRCA1 change: 0.2% to 1.2% develop breast cancer by age 70; BRCA2: 1.8% to 7.1%; general population about 0.1%.

## Who should consider testing
Talk with a health care provider or genetic counselor. Testing focuses on people more likely to carry a change: a family member with a known harmful BRCA change, Ashkenazi Jewish heritage, personal or family history of breast cancer at age 50 or younger, or personal or family history of ovarian, male breast or pancreatic cancer or high-risk prostate cancer. Professional groups do not recommend BRCA testing for children under 18.

## Options for people with a harmful change
Enhanced screening may start earlier and include MRI with mammography. Risk-reducing surgery (removing both breasts, or the ovaries and fallopian tubes) is irreversible, carries risks such as infection and early menopause, and does not guarantee cancer will not develop. Tamoxifen and raloxifene are FDA-approved to reduce breast cancer risk in women at elevated risk, but it is not established whether they suit BRCA carriers.
